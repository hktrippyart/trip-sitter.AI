# Module 2: Trauma-Attuned Care & Cultural Consideration

(Principle 1: Creating a Safe Space)

## Core Module Objectives

This module explores the core foundational principle of peer support: "Creating a Safe Space." During moments of acute distress, sensory overload, or heightened altered states, psychological defenses are significantly lowered. Unresolved trauma, past experiences of oppression, or deep-seated fears can surface rapidly.

This module prepares volunteers to apply the core concepts of Trauma-Informed Care and Cultural Attunement to cultivate a container of spaciousness, radical acceptance, and respect for diverse backgrounds, actively preventing re-traumatization during the peer support process.

## 1. Trauma-Informed Principles

### 1.1 Understanding Trauma & Preventing Re-Traumatization

The Nature of Trauma: Trauma is not merely the event itself, but the nervous system's overwhelmed response to the event. In a state of intense distress or confusion, a guest may struggle to differentiate between a past traumatic memory and their present environment.

Preventing Re-Traumatization — Avoid Coercion and Control: Forcing a guest to sit down, lie down, speak, or remain in a specific area can trigger implicit memories of past helplessness, restraint, or loss of autonomy.

Preventing Re-Traumatization — Avoid Pathologizing: Never label deep emotional discharges (such as weeping, shaking, or physical tremors) as "pathological" or "out of control."

### 1.2 The Art of Invitational Language

Trauma strips individuals of their agency; therefore, a volunteer's language must center on choice and empowerment.

Directive vs. Invitational Language, example 1. Directive (avoid): "Sit down right now and drink this water." Invitational (use): "If you'd like, there's a chair available here, or you are welcome to stay standing. I also have water here whenever you feel ready."

Directive vs. Invitational Language, example 2. Directive (avoid): "Take a deep breath and calm down." Invitational (use): "If it feels okay for you, we can try bringing our attention to our feet on the ground, or take a gentle exhale together."

Transparency & Intent: Always inform and request permission before making shifts in the environment or physical space (e.g., "I'm going to grab a blanket for you, is that alright?").

### 1.3 Non-Verbal Presence & Co-Regulation

Nervous systems communicate directly with one another. A volunteer's internal stability acts as a physiological anchor of safety for the guest.

Body Posture & Physical Proximity: Maintain an open, non-threatening posture (e.g., body slightly angled, hands relaxed and visible, eye level at or slightly below the guest's). Continuously respect the guest's personal space boundaries, avoiding standing directly over them or behind them, which can feel intimidating or overwhelming.

Paralanguage Regulation: Slow down your speech rate, lower your vocal pitch, and soften your tone. Calming vocal cadence sends implicit cues of safety to the guest's nervous system.

## 2. Cultural Attunement & Intersectionality

### 2.1 Social Location & Implicit Bias

A volunteer's background (race, gender, class, sexual orientation, disability status, etc.) shapes how they perceive and interact with a guest.

Awareness of Power Dynamics: In a support dynamic, volunteers naturally occupy a position of authority/clarity. Volunteers holding privileged social identities must exercise vigilance to avoid unintentionally projecting authority, judgment, or condescension.

Reflecting on Implicit Bias: Notice personal assumptions or moral judgments regarding a guest's background, clothing, expression style, or intense emotional displays (such as anger or loud grieving).

### 2.2 Cross-Cultural Support & DEAI (Diversity, Equity, Accessibility, Inclusion)

Cultural Humility: Recognize that one can never fully understand every aspect of another person's lived cultural experience. Refrain from imposing personal cultural interpretations; maintain curiosity and respect.

Avoiding Microaggressions: Avoid making identity-based assumptions, intrusive personal questions, or comments about a guest's appearance or demeanor.

Gender-Affirming Care: Respect the guest's self-identified pronouns. If unsure, use gender-neutral language or politely ask for their preferred name/pronouns when appropriate.

Accessibility & Neurodiversity: Consider sensory and physical needs, including physical space accessibility and managing sensory overload (e.g., dimming lights, offering earplugs, reducing noise). Understand that neurodivergent individuals (e.g., autistic or ADHD individuals) in heightened emotional states may process and express experiences differently. Do not force traditional eye contact or conversational norms.

## 3. Rupture and Repair

In heightened or vulnerable settings, volunteers—despite good intentions—may misstep, misread cues, or trigger discomfort in a guest. Knowing how to navigate relational "ruptures" is vital to trauma-attuned care.

### 3.1 Intent vs. Impact

Intent: The volunteer's internal motivation (e.g., "I was just trying to help").

Impact: The guest's lived experience of the interaction (e.g., "That felt intrusive/unsafe to me").

Core Rule: When a rupture occurs, prioritize the Impact over defending the Intent. Avoid defensive statements like: "I was only trying to help you, you don't need to react that way."

### 3.2 Four Steps to Relational Repair

Step 1 — Pause & Notice: Recognize signs of defense, withdrawal, discomfort, or anger in the guest.

Step 2 — Drop Defensiveness: Set aside the urge to explain or justify your actions.

Step 3 — Acknowledge & Apologize. Example: "Thank you for telling me. I'm sorry for standing too close / saying that, and for making you feel uncomfortable."

Step 4 — Re-negotiate & Invite. Example: "Would you prefer I step back a few feet? Or is there a different way you'd like me to support you right now?"

### 3.3 Dynamic Maintenance of Personal Boundaries

Bi-Directional Boundaries: Trauma-attuned care does not mean accepting abusive or unsafe behaviors. If a guest crosses physical or verbal boundaries (e.g., unconsented touching, physical aggression, verbal abuse), volunteers must set clear boundaries using kind and firm communication. Example: "I am very glad to sit with you, but for both of our safety, I need you to keep a little distance / I cannot let you grab my arm."
