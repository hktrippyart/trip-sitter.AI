# Module 4: Navigating Difficult Experiences & Crisis Intervention

(Principles 3 & 4: Embracing the Process / Reframing Challenges)

## Core Module Objectives

This module explores two core principles of peer support: "Embracing the Process (Through, Not Down)" and "Reframing Challenges (Difficult Is Not Necessarily Bad)."

During moments of heightened altered states, acute sensory overload, or intense stress, physical and emotional responses—such as weeping, fear, repetitive thoughts, or somatic shaking—are often mislabeled as dangerous or negative states that must be immediately suppressed.

This module trains volunteers to reframe challenging emotional experiences, master non-verbal de-escalation techniques, recognize the scope of peer boundaries, and accurately identify medical or psychiatric emergencies to execute protocolized escalations.

## 1. Reframing Challenges & "Through, Not Down"

### 1.1 Reframing Hard Experiences

Reframing Challenges: Extreme fear, grief, anger, or bodily discomfort often signify the natural processing and release of suppressed emotional stress, unassimilated experiences, or dissolving coping mechanisms.

Avoiding Forced Suppression: Traditional responses often attempt to "talk down," force sedation, or overly control an individual to suppress their process. Peer support advocates for "Navigating Through"—providing a calm, secure container so the guest can safely process and move through the experience at their own pace.

### 1.2 Differentiating a "Difficult Journey" from an "Actual Crisis"

Volunteers must discern whether a guest is undergoing an intense psychological process or facing an actual safety/medical crisis. The four evaluation dimensions are Primary Driver, Reality Orientation, Safety Threat, and Intervention Approach.

Primary Driver — Difficult Journey: emotional peaks, inner processing, somatic discharge. Actual Harm / Crisis: medical emergency, severe psychosis, physical violence.

Reality Orientation — Difficult Journey: fearful or disoriented, but retains basic connection. Actual Harm / Crisis: complete detachment, unresponsiveness, severe delusions.

Safety Threat — Difficult Journey: no intent or action to harm self or others. Actual Harm / Crisis: immediate threat of self-harm, aggression, or violence.

Intervention Approach — Difficult Journey: sit with, ground, offer space and emotional containment. Actual Harm / Crisis: activate crisis escalation, involve medical/safety teams.

### 1.3 Supporting Somatic & Emotional Discharge

Allowing Physiological Release: Crying, shaking, shivering, temperature fluctuations, fetal positioning, or repetitive movements are natural ways the nervous system regulates and releases stress.

Volunteer's Response — Do not interrupt or rush to comfort: Avoid immediately shoving a tissue or forcing a hug, which can interrupt their organic process or feel intrusive.

Volunteer's Response — Maintain a grounded presence: Provide cushions, blankets, or spatial adjustments for physical safety so the guest does not accidentally injure themselves during release.

## 2. Challenging Expressions & De-Escalation Techniques

### 2.1 Common Difficult Expressions & Counterstrategies

Paranoia & Excessive Insecurity — Presentation: Suspecting the volunteer has malicious intent, expressing irrational fear of surroundings, or feeling extreme insecurity. Strategy: Do not argue with or validate false beliefs; maintain complete transparency, state your intentions clearly before moving, and offer ample physical space.

Thought Looping — Presentation: Repeating the same phrase, question, or physical action obsessively due to intense anxiety. Strategy: Avoid debating the logic of the loop. Gently shift sensory input (e.g., offer a sip of water, suggest changing sitting position, or bring awareness to their feet on the floor).

High Arousal & Restlessness — Presentation: Extreme restlessness, urge to pace, or intense physical agitation. Strategy: Provide spaciousness. As long as there is no immediate danger, allow them to move around safely within the designated area; avoid physically blocking their path.

### 2.2 Non-Verbal De-escalation

When a guest is highly aroused or potentially defensive, a volunteer's body language is the primary tool for de-escalation.

Maintain Physical Distance: Keep a distance of at least 6 feet (around 1.8 meters) to respect personal space and avoid triggering feelings of confinement.

Stance & Body Language — 45-Degree Angle Stance: Avoid standing directly face-to-face (which can feel confrontational). Angle your body slightly sideways with feet shoulder-width apart. Hands Relaxed & Visible: Keep hands open and at chest/waist level, signaling non-aggression and transparency.

Keep Exits Unblocked: Ensure that both the volunteer and the guest have an unobstructed path to an exit so no one feels trapped or cornered.

## 3. Crisis Screening & Escalation Protocols

When a situation exceeds the scope of peer sitting (e.g., medical collapse, violent behavior, severe psychiatric breakdown), escalation protocols must be activated immediately.

Scene & Risk Screening Flow: Medical / Psych Emergency → Call Emergency Services (999) & Event Medical Support. Extreme Violence / Self-Harm → Retreat to Safety / Contact Event Security / Safety Team.

### 3.1 Medical Red Flags

If any of the following symptoms occur, immediately contact event medical staff or local emergency services (999): loss of consciousness, inability to awaken, or unresponsiveness to verbal/physical stimuli; severe breathing distress (hyperventilating, choking, or fewer than 8 breaths per minute); severe seizures or involuntary convulsions; signs of heatstroke/hyperthermia (extremely hot, dry skin) or severe dehydration; chest pain, persistent arrhythmia, vomiting blood, or sudden physical collapse.

### 3.2 Psychiatric Emergencies & Risk Assessment

Assessing Suicidality: Ask directly and gently: "Are you having thoughts of hurting yourself or ending your life right now?" Evaluate the Three Factors: Plan, Means, and Intent. If these factors are present, this is a high-risk emergency requiring continuous safety monitoring and immediate escalation to medical/psychiatric professionals or crisis hotlines.

Distinguishing Extreme Confusion from Reality Loss: Observe if the guest has completely lost real-world testing capabilities, exhibits severe and persistent ungrounded hallucinations, or poses destructive behavior that cannot be anchored via grounding techniques.

### 3.3 Escalation Resources & Handoff Protocols

Mandatory Team Lead Escalation for External Referrals: Unless facing an immediate life-threatening situation (where emergency services like 999 must be contacted directly), volunteers must escalate and inform team leads first for any non-life-threatening situations before initiating or advising on any external referrals.

Hong Kong Crisis Support Resources: In non-life-threatening but severe emotional crisis situations, volunteers can guide guests or family members toward local professional support services (e.g., The Samaritan Befrienders Hong Kong, Caritas Family Crisis Support Centre, or hospital emergency departments).

Objective Information Handoff: When transferring care to medical, psychiatric, or event safety personnel, provide concise, objective facts (timeline of events, observed behaviors/symptoms, and support interventions attempted) without subjective speculation, moral judgment, or legal assumptions.
