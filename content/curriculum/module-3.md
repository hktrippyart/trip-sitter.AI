# Module 3: Non-Directive Inquiry & Active Listening

(Principle 2: Presence & Non-Directive Support)

## Core Module Objectives

This module dives into the second foundational principle of peer support: "Presence & Non-Directive Support" (Sitting, Not Guiding). During intense emotional states, acute stress, or heightened altered states, guests are exceptionally susceptible to external verbal and non-verbal suggestions. A volunteer is not a therapist, counselor, or interpreter; their mission is to serve as a grounded, stable "container" while trusting the guest's own Inner Resiliency & Wisdom.

This module equips volunteers with skills in non-directive communication, reflective listening, structured summarizing, and somatic awareness—teaching volunteers how to invite curiosity rather than provide answers.

## 1. Presence & Non-Directive Support (Core Mindset)

### 1.1 Trusting Inner Resiliency & Wisdom

Core Philosophy: An individual's mind and nervous system possess an innate orientation toward self-integration and healing (similar to how the physical body heals a cut). A volunteer does not need to "fix" or "rescue" the guest—only to provide a safe, non-threatening environment for their organic process to unfold naturally.

Beginner's Mind: Set aside the posture of an "expert" or "analyst." Refrain from diagnosing, judging, or categorizing the guest's experience; maintain pure curiosity and respect for their unique personal journey.

### 1.2 Relinquishing Agendas & Expectations

No Personal Agenda: Volunteers must continuously monitor their own implicit expectations (e.g., "I hope they have an emotional breakthrough," or "I hope they get clarity on their life"). Trying to push a guest toward a specific "epiphany" or outcome is a subtle form of control and power projection.

Allowing Non-Linearity & Disorganization: A guest's internal process may appear chaotic, illogical, repetitive, or seemingly stagnant. The volunteer's task is to be with the state as it is, rather than trying to fix, organize, or streamline it.

## 2. Reflective Listening & Summarizing Techniques

The essence of non-directive communication lies not in offering advice or solutions, but in serving as a clear, warm mirror—helping the guest hear their own inner voice.

### 2.1 Paraphrasing & Emotional Reflection

Function: To validate the guest's experience, ensure they feel heard and seen, and avoid introducing the volunteer's personal interpretations.

Technique — Emotional Reflection: Capture the underlying emotional tone of the guest's words. Example: "It sounds like seeing that image felt deeply lonely and terrifying for you."

Technique — Content Reflection: Restate the core of the narrative using the guest's own key words or neutral terms. Example: "You feel like you're stuck in a closed room with no visible exit."

### 2.2 Structuring Summaries

When a guest shares a complex, non-linear, or overwhelming amount of information, the volunteer can use a structured summary to help them organize their thoughts. The Three-Step Summary Structure is:

Step 1 — Framing: "Let me see if I am understanding you correctly..."

Step 2 — Core Essence: Summarize key elements (Emotion + Somatic Sensation + Main Image/Theme).

Step 3 — Check-In Invitation: "Did I get that right?" or "Is there more to that?"

## 3. Non-Directive Inquiry & Somatic Awareness

When a guest feels stuck in an intellectual thought loop or asks the volunteer for answers, the volunteer uses Non-Directive Inquiry to return agency and ownership back to the guest.

### 3.1 Open-Ended vs. Closed/Leading Questions

Avoid Closed or Leading Questions. Example to avoid: "Are you sad because of something from your past?" (Diagnostic and leading.) Example to avoid: "Are you feeling a little better now?" (Implies pressure on the guest to feel better.)

Use Open-Ended Questions. Example: "As that thought comes up, what are you experiencing right now?" (Focuses on the present moment.) Example: "How would you like us to support you right now?" (Offers choice and agency.) Example: "If that fear had a voice, what might it be saying?" (Invites curiosity toward inner experience.)

### 3.2 Handling Requests for Advice or Interpretation

When a guest asks: "What should I do?" or "What does this mean?", reflect back to their inner wisdom. Example: "That's a meaningful question. When you pause with it, what intuition or body sense comes up for you?" Example: "I don't know what it means either, but we can stay right here and explore what that feeling is bringing up for you."

### 3.3 Somatic Awareness & Grounding

Intense emotional or psychological experiences are often deeply physical. Volunteers can gently guide guests from over-intellectualizing (cognitive looping) back to direct bodily felt sense (somatic presence).

Somatic Inquiry: "As you talk about that, where do you notice sensations in your body right now? (e.g., tightness in the chest, warmth in the stomach)" and "Can we bring a little space to that spot and see what it might need?"

Gentle Grounding — Sensory Anchors: Direct attention to physical contact points (e.g., feeling the texture of a blanket, sipping warm water, feeling feet touching the floor).

Gentle Grounding — Visual Anchors: Invite the guest to pick a stable object in the room (e.g., a chair, a plant) and describe its color and shape.
