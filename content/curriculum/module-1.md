# Module 1: Ethics, Safety Foundations & Volunteer Self-Care

## Core Module Objectives

This module provides peer support volunteers with a strong foundation in scope of practice and boundaries, physical and psychological safety assessment, and self-regulation.

Before providing peer-based support or compassionate listening, a volunteer must understand their boundaries, evaluate environmental risks, and maintain personal nervous system regulation when accompanying individuals in non-ordinary or heightened emotional states (e.g., severe sensory overload, acute distress, or altered states of awareness).

## 1. Scope of Practice & Ethics

### 1.1 Peer Role & Non-Hierarchical Relationship

Horizontal Relationship: The connection between a volunteer and a guest is a person-to-person relationship, not a hierarchical expert-to-patient dynamic.

Non-Medical / Non-Therapeutic Position: Volunteers do not act as psychiatrists, counselors, social workers, or emergency medical services (EMS). The objective is solely to provide a safe space and a grounded, supportive presence—not to diagnose, treat, or deliver clinical interventions.

Non-Directive Approach: Volunteers do not actively guide, fix, or direct the guest's process, nor do they impose personal beliefs, advice, or therapeutic goals.

Awareness of Dual Relationships: Volunteers must be consciously aware of any potential dual relationships (e.g., pre-existing personal, professional, romantic, or social connections with a guest) that could compromise the objective, peer-to-peer dynamic. If there is any uncertainty regarding a dual relationship or boundary crossover, volunteers must inform team leads immediately before proceeding with support.

### 1.2 Scope Boundaries (What Volunteers DO & DO NOT Do)

What Volunteers DO: Provide a safe physical environment and a grounded, compassionate presence. Offer active listening, reflective support, and simple grounding techniques. Offer basic comfort care (e.g., water, blankets, tissues). Recognize and escalate medical or psychiatric emergencies to appropriate professional personnel.

What Volunteers DO NOT Do — No Diagnosing, Prescribing, or Substance Handling: Volunteers strictly do not diagnose conditions, recommend treatments, or facilitate, handle, supply, or advise on any controlled medications, substances, or chemicals.

What Volunteers DO NOT Do — No Physical Restraint: Volunteers never use physical force, physical restraint, or coercive verbal holding (unless responding to an immediate, life-threatening self-defense situation).

What Volunteers DO NOT Do — No Unsolicited Psychological Analysis: Volunteers avoid psychoanalyzing or interpreting the guest's experience while the guest is in a vulnerable state.

### 1.3 Ethical Boundaries & Consent

Strict Prohibition of Exploitative Conduct: Any form of sexual harassment, sexual contact, or sexually suggestive language/behavior is strictly prohibited.

Navigating Vulnerability & Boundary Confusion: Guests in heightened altered states may experience intense vulnerability or idealization toward the helper. Volunteers must maintain clear professional boundaries and never exploit a guest's emotional state.

Touch & Consent Boundaries — Explicit Verbal Consent: Any physical contact (e.g., holding a hand, offering a hug, touching a shoulder) requires clear, explicit verbal consent beforehand (e.g., "Is it okay if I hold your hand?").

Touch & Consent Boundaries — Dynamic Withdrawal: Consent can be withdrawn at any time. If a guest hesitates, pulls back, or is unable to give informed consent (e.g., severely disoriented or incapacitated), volunteers must maintain physical distance.

Confidentiality: Information shared by a guest during a session remains strictly confidential. Exceptions: Confidentiality is breached only in cases of immediate harm to self, harm to others, or severe medical emergencies requiring professional medical intervention.

## 2. Environment & Situation Safety

### 2.1 Three Pillars of Safety

Physical Safety: Ensuring the space is free from sharp objects, dangerous structures, fall hazards, extreme temperatures, or vehicle traffic.

Emotional Safety: Creating an accepting, non-judgmental atmosphere where guests feel safe expressing vulnerability or intense emotions without fear of shame.

Psychological Safety: Ensuring the guest retains personal agency and knows they will not be coerced, judged, or punished.

### 2.2 Situation Safety Assessment Protocol

Before entering or intervening in a support scenario, volunteers must perform a dynamic assessment in three steps: Scene Size-Up, Volunteer Safety First, and Evaluating Guest State & Bystanders.

Step 1 — Scene Size-Up: Are there immediate physical hazards (fire, broken glass, heights, moving vehicles)? Are there dangerous objects present? Where are the exits? Is there a safe retreat route if the situation escalates?

Step 2 — Volunteer Safety First. Core Principle: If a volunteer's own safety is compromised, they cannot effectively help anyone else. Volunteer safety is always the primary priority. Buddy System: Never handle an escalated, potentially aggressive, or highly unpredictable situation alone. Always work in pairs or stay in direct communication with the support team.

Step 3 — Evaluating Guest State & Bystanders: Are well-meaning friends or crowds creating unnecessary sensory stimulation or distress for the guest? Is there a need to move to a quieter, low-stimulation de-escalation zone away from bright lights, loud noises, or onlookers?

## 3. Volunteer Self-Tracking & Nervous System Regulation

In peer support work, the volunteer's own nervous system acts as a primary tool. Through co-regulation, a guest's dysregulated nervous system intuitively looks to the volunteer for cues of safety and stability.

### 3.1 Nervous System & The Window of Tolerance

Window of Tolerance: The optimal zone where a volunteer remains grounded, calm, empathetic, and mentally clear.

Hyperarousal: Triggered by high-stress or intense environments; marked by rapid heart rate, muscle tension, anxiety, or an urge to control the situation (Fight/Flight).

Hypoarousal: Triggered by overwhelm or exhaustion; marked by numbness, difficulty focusing, dissociation, or emotional withdrawal (Freeze/Fawn).

The three states in summary: Hyperarousal (above the window) shows as anxiety, racing heart, and control urges. The Window of Tolerance (the middle zone) shows as calm, present, empathetic, and co-regulating. Hypoarousal (below the window) shows as numbness, fatigue, and dissociation.

### 3.2 Real-Time Self-Tracking: The W.A.I.T. Principle

During support sessions, volunteers should continually pause and internally ask: W.A.I.T. — Why Am I Talking? Is what I am saying serving the guest, or is it easing my own discomfort/anxiety? Am I attempting to "fix," direct, or intellectualize their experience?

Rule of Thumb: When in doubt, default to quiet, grounded presence.

### 3.3 In-the-Moment Micro-Regulations

Somatic Grounding: Shift awareness down to the feet making contact with the floor; feel the support of gravity and the ground.

Physiological Sigh: Take two quick double-inhalations through the nose followed by a long exhale through the mouth to activate the parasympathetic nervous system.

Spatial Micro-Adjustments: If feeling overwhelmed by a guest's energy, subtly shift posture or take a half-step back to preserve personal boundaries.

### 3.4 Preventing Vicarious Traumatization & Burnout

Awareness of Compassion Fatigue: Repeated exposure to others' distress or crisis can lead to emotional exhaustion if left unchecked.

Shift Rotations & Duty Limits: Adhere to reasonable shift lengths (e.g., 4–6 hours) and conduct thorough handoffs. Recognize when you are falling out of your Window of Tolerance, and ask a teammate to step in.

Debriefing & De-roping: Participate in post-shift debriefs with peers or team leaders to process absorbed stress. Practice intentional transition rituals (e.g., washing hands, changing clothes, taking a walk) to step out of the volunteer role and return to everyday life.
